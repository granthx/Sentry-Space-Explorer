Exoplanet Finder

When a planet crosses in front of its star, the star dims by a tiny fraction. A 1-D CNN reads NASA Kepler light curves and learns to separate a real planet's dip from look-alikes like eclipsing binaries — the same approach Google researchers used to discover Kepler-90i.

How it works

  1. Light curve fetch — The Kepler Space Telescope measured stellar brightness every 29.4 minutes for ~4 years. Each dip pattern is downloaded from the NASA Exoplanet Archive.
  2. Normalisation — Flux values are median-normalised and binned to 178 evenly spaced time steps, removing stellar variability.
  3. 1-D CNN — A convolutional network scans the binned flux array and outputs a probability that the dip pattern belongs to a genuine planetary transit.
  4. Verdict — The model outputs PLANET or FALSE POSITIVE with a confidence score. False positives include eclipsing binaries, instrumental noise and background stars.

Google researchers used a similar CNN approach in 2017 to discover Kepler-90i, the eighth planet in the Kepler-90 system (KIC 11442793).
